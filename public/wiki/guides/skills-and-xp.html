Character progression

Skills & XP

Skills define what your character can become good at; XP and learning systems are how you invest in that identity. The strongest long-term characters are usually not the ones that buy every available skill immediately, but the ones whose development supports the activities they repeatedly perform.

Beginner–IntermediateLong-term progressionNo hidden formula spoilers

In brief

Choose a core role, improve the abilities that feed that role, use education and practice to support it, then branch out when a second skill genuinely opens new opportunities. Skill alone rarely decides an outcome: preparation, song familiarity, condition, equipment, collaborators and context can matter too.

How to think about skills

Core musical ability

Instrument and vocal skills are the foundation for performing the roles your character actually takes on in songs, rehearsals, recordings and gigs.

Creative ability

Songwriting and related creative skills support the quality and flexibility of the material you create.

Performance ability

Playing accurately is only part of live music. Stage-oriented skills help turn technical ability into a stronger show.

Specialist careers

Production, teaching, media, business and other careers can reward characters who deliberately build a second professional identity.

XP is an investment decision

XP lets you progress, but spending it without a plan can leave you with many shallow abilities and no clear strength. Before spending, ask what the skill will let you do that your current build cannot do well enough.

  1. Choose the activity. Decide whether you are improving performance, songwriting, recording, teaching or another career.
  2. Identify the relevant skill path. Follow the skill tree rather than buying nearby skills simply because they are available.
  3. Build usable depth. A role becomes valuable when it is strong enough to matter consistently in real activities.
  4. Add complementary skills. Expand once the new ability supports something you already do or unlocks a new route you genuinely want.
Mechanics without spoilersThe Compendium does not publish an XP-per-outcome conversion or a mathematically best purchase order. Skill effectiveness depends on the activity and the rest of the situation.

Learning is more than spending XP

Lessons, tutorials, university-style study, tutors, teaching and practice can all sit around the core skill system. These routes create trade-offs between money, time and flexibility.

RouteGood forTrade-off
PracticeRegular development tied closely to music activityConsumes time you could use elsewhere
Structured educationPlanned progressionMay cost money and schedule space
Tutoring/teachingPlayer-to-player or specialist learningDepends on availability and suitability
Real activityPutting abilities into contextPreparation and other factors still matter

Specialise first, broaden second

A useful pattern for a musician is role → reliability → versatility. Become dependable at the thing your band expects you to do, then add secondary abilities that solve real problems: extra vocals, another instrument family, songwriting, production or a parallel career.

ExampleA guitarist who performs, rehearses and records regularly will normally get more value from deepening relevant guitar and performance abilities before investing heavily in an unrelated career branch.

Common progression mistakes

  • Buying skills because they are visible rather than because they support a plan.
  • Ignoring prerequisite or parent skills in the skill tree.
  • Expecting one high skill to cancel poor preparation or wellness.
  • Spreading across too many instruments before becoming dependable at one role.
  • Failing to revisit your build after joining a band or changing career direction.
